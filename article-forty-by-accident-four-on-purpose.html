Forty by Accident, Four on Purpose

Share

About a week into a strict stretch of eating only vegetables and meat, Joseph ordered a takeaway coffee and the cafe put a sugar in it by mistake. One teaspoon. Within a short time he felt itchy and genuinely unwell, what he describes as feeling it in his blood, and it stopped him in his tracks. Back in his office days after university, someone would bring cake to the desk most afternoons and he felt nothing, because sugar was the baseline of everything he ate, and you only notice the water when you climb out of it.

The film that changed how we read a label

In 2014, Australian filmmaker Damon Gameau made a documentary called That Sugar Film, and if you have not seen it, we would gently suggest you stop reading this article, watch it tonight, and come back tomorrow. It streams on Netflix and Prime Video in Australia, and the official trailer is on YouTube. The short version is this: Gameau ate forty teaspoons of sugar a day for sixty days, which is roughly one hundred and sixty grams and sits almost exactly on the Australian daily average at the time, and he did it without touching a single soft drink, block of chocolate, ice cream or lolly.

Every teaspoon came from foods that were sold to him, and to the rest of us, as healthy choices: low-fat flavoured yoghurt, breakfast cereal, muesli bars, fruit juice, pasta sauces and condiments. One of the more startling moments in the film is his walk down the sauce aisle, where he discovers that BBQ sauce, hoisin sauce and sweet chilli sauce each carry more sugar per serve than chocolate sauce. That is not a quirk of one brand. It is almost the category norm.

The line from the film that has stayed with us ever since is his point on moderation. One chocolate bar, eaten consciously as a treat, does feel moderate. But if it is your fortieth teaspoon of the day, hidden across your cereal, your juice, your yoghurt and your sandwich sauce, then moderation is not what is actually happening. The number is the same either way. The awareness is the only thing that differs.

The film sent Joseph straight to the back of the pack. We were already a lower-sugar household by that point, but we had not been reading everything carefully. A tub of flavoured yoghurt that had been going into the kids' lunchboxes as the healthy option turned out to hold around sixteen grams of sugar, which is four teaspoons, in a single serve. That one label started a habit of reading every pack before it comes into the house, and it has not stopped since.

What sugar actually does once it is inside you

To understand why this matters, it helps to spend a few minutes on the mechanics, because the way fructose behaves in the body is genuinely different from the way other carbohydrates behave, and most people have never been told why.

Every starchy or sugary food you eat eventually becomes glucose in your bloodstream. Glucose is the body's main fuel, and insulin, the hormone your pancreas releases, moves that glucose into your cells to be burned or stored as needed. That part is fairly well understood. What is less well known is what happens with fructose, the other half of the sugar molecule [1][2].

Table sugar, the kind in your coffee or your biscuit, is made of two parts joined together: one part glucose and one part fructose. Fruit juice, honey, high-fructose corn syrup and most added sugars follow a similar pattern. When you eat them, the fructose travels to the liver, and almost all of it is handled there, bypassing the normal insulin system entirely [4]. When the liver receives more fructose than it can use for energy, it converts the excess directly into fat, a process called de novo lipogenesis, which simply means making new fat from scratch [2][3]. Over time, that fat builds up in the liver itself, and this is how a diet high in added sugar contributes to fatty liver disease, even in people who drink no alcohol at all [1][6].

This is why a glass of apple juice is not the same thing as a baked potato, even though both will raise your blood sugar. The potato is almost entirely starch, which breaks down to glucose and follows the normal insulin pathway. The juice is roughly half fructose, and that half goes straight to the liver to be processed in an entirely different way [4][5]. One is a slow, regulated fuel. The other is, in excess, a fast track to liver fat. The research on this is now very well established [7][8].

Fructose also raises uric acid in the blood as it is metabolised, and elevated uric acid is linked to insulin resistance and a higher risk of metabolic disease, a connection that researchers have been working to understand for over a decade [14]. On top of all of that, added sugar feeds the wrong bacteria in both the mouth and the gut, disrupting the community of microorganisms that keep digestion and immunity working well [15]. We have written more about what happens to the gut when its bacterial balance is thrown off in our gut health article, which is worth reading alongside this one. For now, the important point is simply this: sugar does not just affect your weight. It affects your liver, your blood chemistry, and the health of your gut, all at the same time.

The numbers most of us are living with

The infographics below are worth pausing on, because seeing the teaspoons laid out visually does something that a paragraph of numbers cannot quite do on its own.

[Infographic 1: The film's breakfast] In That Sugar Film, Gameau sits down to a breakfast that most Australian families would recognise: a bowl of breakfast cereal at around seven and a half teaspoons of sugar, a tub of low-fat flavoured yoghurt at four teaspoons, and a four-hundred millilitre glass of apple juice at somewhere between nine and eleven teaspoons [13]. That is approximately twenty teaspoons before he leaves the table, which is already half of the forty-teaspoon daily average the film documents. One teaspoon is four grams, so if you are reading a nutrition label, divide the grams of sugar by four and that is how many teaspoons are sitting in your hand.

[Infographic 2: Common foods in teaspoons] Added sugar per serve: a 375 ml can of soft drink carries around ten teaspoons, a 250 ml glass of apple juice around six, a tub of flavoured yoghurt around four, a muesli bar around three, two tablespoons of sweet chilli sauce around three, and two tablespoons of BBQ sauce around two [13]. The grey row in the graphic is different: starchy foods are not added sugar, but they do become glucose in the body, so one cup of cooked white rice is roughly equivalent to eight teaspoons of glucose, a medium baked potato to around six, and two slices of white bread to around four. They are not fructose-driven in the same way, but they still matter to the overall picture.

When you add up an ordinary day honestly, the number lands somewhere around forty teaspoons for many Australian adults [13]. The World Health Organisation recommends keeping free sugars to less than ten per cent of your total daily energy intake, which for most adults works out to around twelve teaspoons or fewer [12]. The gap between where most of us are and where the evidence suggests we should be is not a small one.

What a real day looks like, side by side

The most useful thing we can show you is a comparison, because forty teaspoons sounds abstract until you see exactly where they come from.

An average day: Breakfast is cereal and a glass of juice, around eighteen teaspoons before 8 am. A muesli bar mid-morning adds three more. Lunch is a sandwich with a generous squeeze of sauce, another three to four. An afternoon flavoured yoghurt brings four more. A soft drink with dinner is another ten. A takeaway meal with a sauce-heavy marinade or glaze adds at least two or three. Add it up honestly and you are at or past forty teaspoons, without a single chocolate bar, without a dessert, and without anything that felt indulgent at the time.

The family's day: Breakfast in our house is eggs most mornings, or a long-fermented sourdough on the days we make it. Water is the only drink, for the adults and the kids, and has been for years: no juice, no soft drink, no kombucha. Lunch and dinner are built around meat and vegetables with no bottled sauces. When we want something sweet in the evening, it is our homemade chocolate pudding or a bowl of vanilla ice cream made from ingredients we have chosen ourselves. Both recipes are on the site and we make them regularly. Joseph never adds sugar to anything, coffee included, and nothing is eaten after six in the evening as a general rule. When we tally our day honestly, including the maple syrup in the pudding and anything else that carries natural sweetness, we land at around four teaspoons, and every one of them was chosen deliberately rather than hidden in something that was supposed to be healthy. That contrast is the headline of this whole conversation.

The shift was not about deprivation. It was about substitution: replacing the packaged reward with the homemade one, replacing the bottled sauce with a simple seasoning, replacing the juice with water. One of our boys was trained off sugar completely in the process, and that was easier than we expected once the house was simply stocked differently.

The risk that builds quietly over time

The reason we care about this beyond the day-to-day is that the health consequences of high added sugar intake do not announce themselves quickly. They accumulate over years, which is exactly what makes them easy to dismiss in the short term.

Repeated research has linked high added sugar consumption to weight gain, elevated triglycerides in the blood, raised blood pressure, a higher risk of type 2 diabetes, and increased cardiovascular disease risk [9][10][11]. A large systematic review found that people who regularly drank sugar-sweetened beverages had a fifty-three per cent greater likelihood of developing fatty liver disease compared with those who did not [6]. Those are not small numbers, and they are not attached to people who were eating what most of us would recognise as an extreme diet. They are attached to ordinary intake levels that many families live with every week.

The liver is the organ that carries most of this burden, quietly and without much warning [2][3]. It does not send pain signals the way a sore knee does. It simply accumulates fat, loses efficiency, and eventually begins to affect the way the whole body manages energy and inflammation. By the time a person notices something is wrong, the process has often been running for a long time [1][7].

We are not trying to frighten anyone, because fear is not a sustainable motivator and it is not really our style. What we are saying is that the research is clear, the mechanism is well understood, and the good news is that reducing added sugar is one of the most straightforward and impactful things a family can do, because so much of the intake is hidden in foods that simply need to be swapped rather than given up entirely [3][13].

What changed when we got serious about it

Joseph's observation since we cut sugar down to that four-teaspoon range is simple and he will tell you himself: managing his weight has been easy for the first time in his adult life, without counting anything and without any real effort. That is his experience and we share it as exactly that, his observation, nothing more.

What we can also say is that the body's ability to notice things changes when the baseline changes. After years of eating this way, one accidental teaspoon in a coffee produced a real and unpleasant response. That is not a sensitivity or an allergy. That is a body that had been trained completely off something, and was finally clear enough to register the signal. Back when sugar was in everything, the signal was always there and always drowned out. You only feel it in your blood when the blood is finally clean enough to notice.

If you want the full picture, told entertainingly and in a way that will genuinely shift how you look at a supermarket trolley, watch That Sugar Film. It is Damon Gameau's story told in his own voice, and it does the job better than any article can. You can find it at thatsugarfilm.com, watch the trailer on YouTube, and stream the full documentary on Netflix or Prime Video in Australia. We think it is one of the most useful ninety minutes a family can spend on the subject of food.

And when the mood for something sweet comes around, which it will, our chocolate pudding and vanilla ice cream are on the site. Four teaspoons, chosen on purpose, tastes better than forty that you never noticed.

The body is a remarkable thing. Given the right conditions, clear of what harms it and nourished by what genuinely serves it, it finds its own balance with very little effort required. Sugar is one of the clearest examples we know of a substance that sits in the way of that balance, quietly and at scale, in foods that were never supposed to carry it. The good news is that once you can see it clearly, the path forward is not that complicated. Read the label. Make the swap. Make the pudding. Start there.
